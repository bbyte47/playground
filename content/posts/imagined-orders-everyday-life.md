*Notes and reflections on segment 6-2 of Yuval Noah Harari's course lecture, “Building Pyramids.” This article summarizes his arguments and illustrative comparisons.*

![An imagined comparison of a medieval household sharing a stone hall and a modern teenager reading in a private bedroom, illustrating Harari's argument that buildings can reinforce social values.](/image/imagined-orders-everyday-life.png)

**Building Pyramids: How Imagined Orders Become Everyday Life**

In [the previous article](/posts/building-pyramids/), Harari argued that large societies depend on shared stories. Laws, political rights, social ranks, and nations allow strangers to cooperate because people recognize a common order.

That leaves a difficult question. If an order depends on belief, what keeps it from falling apart?

Harari's answer reaches beyond kings and armies. He asks us to look at ordinary life: the rooms we grow up in, the clothes we wear, the holidays we dream about, and the institutions we expect other people to respect.

In his account, these experiences continually reinforce the stories that organize society.

**Force Needs Believers**

Armies, police, courts, and prisons can compel people to obey rules. But Harari argues that force alone cannot sustain a complex social order indefinitely.

Some people must sincerely accept the beliefs behind it. Religious institutions need believers; a democracy needs people committed to its political principles; an economic system depends on people who trust its rules and expectations.

This helps explain why Harari does not describe an imagined order as a deception knowingly imposed by a few people on everyone else. Those administering an institution may believe in it as deeply as the people subject to its authority.

Education and socialization help reproduce that belief. Children encounter their society's assumptions through stories, songs, schools, clothing, and the way adults behave. Over time, a particular arrangement can become so familiar that it seems simply to be how the world works.

Harari develops three explanations for that durability.

**1. Beliefs Take Shape in the Material World**

A story becomes harder to question when people live inside surroundings shaped by it.

Harari uses architecture to explain this. In his comparison, the modern ideal of individualism finds expression in a child's private bedroom. A door, personal possessions, and some freedom to decorate the room give physical form to the idea that a person has an inner life and a space of their own.

Even the expectation that a parent should knock before entering can reinforce that lesson. The value is learned through an everyday arrangement, rather than only through something a teacher says.

Harari contrasts this with a medieval noble household organized around shared halls, public reputation, and a person's place in a hierarchy. In his example, growing up among other people continually reinforces the importance of how the household sees and judges you.

These are broad historical contrasts used to explain his argument, rather than a description of every medieval household or every modern family. The point is that buildings can help teach values. Once people inhabit those buildings, their daily routines can make the values feel natural.

Clothing serves a similar purpose in the lecture. Harari interprets the adoption of working clothes such as jeans by wealthy people as one way an ideal of equality can appear in everyday behavior. His interest is in how social beliefs become visible and familiar through ordinary objects.

**2. The Order Shapes What We Want**

Harari's second explanation concerns desires.

We often experience our wishes as intensely personal. A larger house, a particular lifestyle, or a journey abroad can seem to express who we truly are. Harari asks how much those ambitions owe to the stories we have learned from others.

His main example is tourism. He describes a combination of two outlooks that he calls **romantic consumerism**.

In the romantic outlook he discusses, a fulfilling life requires varied experiences: unfamiliar places, different foods, new relationships, and a wider range of emotions. Consumerism adds the expectation that buying products and services will make life better.

Together, these ideas turn travel into something more than transportation and accommodation. A holiday promises an experience that will broaden or transform the person taking it.

Harari makes the contrast memorable through an imagined comparison between a wealthy modern traveler and an ancient Egyptian elite concerned with a magnificent tomb. He is illustrating how different cultures can direct ambition toward different projects. He is not establishing a universal explanation for why people travel, or documenting a particular ancient couple's decisions.

The question is why one project feels desirable in the first place. A person can pursue a goal with complete sincerity while drawing that goal from a shared cultural framework.

This makes the imagined order especially durable in Harari's account. It helps define not only what people are allowed to do, but also what they hope to achieve.

**3. Belief Is Shared Across Many Minds**

The third explanation brings us back to a distinction introduced in the [Brief History Introduction](/posts/brief_history/): objective, subjective, and intersubjective realities.

An objective phenomenon exists independently of belief. Harari uses radioactivity as an example. A subjective experience depends on an individual mind, as with a child's imaginary companion.

An intersubjective reality depends on a network of people recognizing something together. In Harari's account, corporations, currencies, and political institutions belong to this category.

This is why one person's change of mind rarely dissolves an institution. A company continues to operate because employees, customers, banks, courts, and many others recognize it. One participant can withdraw belief while the wider network remains intact.

Calling an institution intersubjective therefore does not mean its effects are unreal. Shared recognition can organize property, employment, authority, and everyday cooperation. Nor does identifying a social basis for rights settle the separate moral question of which rights people ought to defend.

**Changing a Story Requires Cooperation Too**

Recognizing an imagined order does not automatically free a person from it.

Suppose people want to dissolve a corporation. Harari points out that they generally appeal to another shared institution, such as a legal system. Changing that legal system may require people to organize around a political community or a different vision of legitimate authority.

The effort to change society therefore depends on the same capacity that sustains it: strangers cooperating around common beliefs.

Harari's argument does not rule out change. It explains why private disbelief and collective transformation are different things. For a new arrangement to take hold, people must coordinate their actions and persuade others to recognize it.

The lecture leaves us with a practical way to examine an institution: look at the surroundings that reinforce it, the ambitions it encourages, and the people whose continued recognition keeps it working.

Harari then raises another problem. Even a society with enough food and widely shared beliefs needs ways to manage information. That becomes the subject of the next segment of “Building Pyramids.”
